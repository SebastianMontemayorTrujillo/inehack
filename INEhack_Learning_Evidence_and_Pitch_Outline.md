# INEhack learning evidence and pitch outline

_Evidence for an original civic adventure and a maximum ten slide presentation_

Core conclusion. Educational games can improve learning in some settings, especially when learning is built into the decisions and supported by useful feedback. That evidence justifies testing this prototype. It does not establish that a cozy art style, this particular game, or a brief intervention will increase electoral participation.

### The claim the presentation can defend

“Proponemos una aventura breve para practicar cómo interpretar datos oficiales y participar en decisiones colectivas. El aprendizaje es una hipótesis que vamos a evaluar; no prometemos aumentar la participación electoral sin evidencia.” This is proposed pitch copy, not a result already observed.

### Use three evidence layers

1. Official CCPC observations establish the historical data context and power the playable tasks. They do not diagnose players’ motives or the causes of nonparticipation.

2. Prior learning research supports the design rationale: meaningful decisions, explanatory feedback, retrieval, and reflection. Effects from other studies are not the prototype’s expected effect size.

3. A proposed evaluation measures this prototype’s usability, learning, transfer, and retention. Turnout impact remains outside the initial study.

### Verified Mexican data context

| CCPC 2024 group | Participation among classified records |
| --- | --- |
| National aggregate | 59.7% |
| Ages 18–19 | 56.8% |
| Ages 20–24 | 48.4% |
| Ages 25–29 | 47.3% |

The denominator is SV + NV within each group, excluding NS. These values are neither each group’s share of all voters nor a percentage of all residents. Fresh calculations from the 32 official CSVs reproduce the rounded values in the June 2025 INE report, pages 16–17. Nationally, NS/LN is 6.4%. Keep missing classification visible. [11–12]

A descriptive age pattern does not show that people lack knowledge, do not care, or need a targeted political message. The proposed audience is adult beginners in the general public and educational facilitators, with beginner-friendly Spanish and no demographic political profiling.

Research checked 23 October 2026. No participant data, completed pilot, learning gain, judge score, or turnout effect for the proposed game is reported in this document. Effect sizes below are standardized differences, not percentage improvements.

## What the broad learning evidence supports

| Study and design | Verified finding | How to use it and its limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clark et al. 2016<br>Meta-analysis of digital games in K–16 education [1] | Games vs nongame conditions: g = 0.33; 95% CI [0.19, 0.48]. 57 unique samples, 209 effect sizes. Enhanced vs standard designs: g = 0.34. | Supports an average learning benefit across varied settings and the importance of design. It does not predict this game’s effect or establish Mexican civic outcomes. |
| Wouters et al. 2013<br>Serious-game meta-analysis [2] | 39 studies, 5,547 participants. Learning d = 0.29; retention d = 0.36. Motivation d = 0.26 was not statistically significant. | Do not say games always motivate. The randomized-comparison subgroup’s learning estimate was d = 0.08, CI [−0.13, 0.29], so design quality matters to interpretation. |
| Wouters and van Oostendorp 2013<br>Instructional support review [3] | 29 studies; 107 comparisons; adjusted N = 3,675. Supported games outperformed unsupported games, d = 0.34. | Supports carefully designed help and reflection. Support types varied and publication-bias signals existed; this is not proof that any tutorial improves learning. |
| Sailer and Homner 2020<br>Gamification meta-analysis [4] | Cognitive g = 0.49; motivational g = 0.36; behavioral learning g = 0.25. The evidence base differs by outcome. | Secondary context for feedback and progress systems. Gamification is not a full adventure game. Behavioral learning outcomes are not real voting behavior; motivation/behavior effects were less stable in rigorous subsets. |

### Interpret effect sizes honestly

A standardized difference compares outcomes in standard-deviation units. g = 0.33 does not mean 33% more learning, a 33-point test gain, or a 33% turnout increase. Different comparators, subjects, learners, exposure times, and outcome tests make a direct ranking of these studies misleading.

Several reviews can include overlapping primary studies. Do not add their sample sizes as independent replications or average their headline effect sizes. Report the population, comparison, uncertainty, and the boundary of the claim next to the number.

### A concise evidence slide

“Los videojuegos educativos pueden apoyar el aprendizaje: g = 0.33 en una revisión de 57 muestras. El apoyo didáctico también importa: d = 0.34 al comparar juegos con y sin apoyo. Son resultados de investigaciones previas, no de nuestro prototipo.” Use Clark et al. and Wouters and van Oostendorp as footnotes, with the confidence interval for the first finding.

## Mechanisms and civic precedents

| Evidence | What was studied | Design implication and boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Habgood and Ainsworth 2011 [5] | A math game with 58 children aged 7–11 in the first study. Integrating learning into gameplay improved learning under fixed time compared with other versions. | Make a correct evidence construction advance the story. This is a design inference from one math-game context, not proof for adult civic learning or a cozy visual style. |
| Yang et al. 2021 [6] | 222 independent classroom studies; N = 48,478. Quizzing improved achievement overall, g = 0.499; effects varied with feedback, repetition, and comparisons. | Revisit a concept later in a new situation. Do not insert a quiz after every action or assume the reported effect transfers to this game. |
| Freeman et al. 2014 [7] | 225 undergraduate STEM studies; assessment performance in 158 studies was 0.47 SD higher under active learning. | Prefer decisions, explanations, and discussion over passive exposition. This is adjacent evidence, not a videogame or civic-education estimate. |
| Roozenbeek and van der Linden 2020 [8] | Randomized N = 681; a brief political-misinformation game vs Tetris. Perceived reliability of manipulative posts declined more in treatment: d = 0.51 for real items, 0.54 for fictitious ones. | Shows a short civic-adjacent game can change measured judgments. It did not measure turnout or observed sharing, and no long-term follow-up was included. |
| Syvertsen et al. 2009 [9] | Randomized semester-long Student Voices program: 1,670 students in 80 classrooms. Specific civic knowledge and self-reported competencies improved. | Use election-related decisions as teachable material. It was not a brief videogame intervention; no unverified effect size or actual-turnout claim is used here. |
| Jerome et al. 2024 [10] | Narrative synthesis of 109 articles on citizenship education and active citizenship. Methods and outcomes were heterogeneous. | Use context-sensitive discussion, investigation, and student voice. This review supplies no pooled causal estimate or universal turnout effect. |

Avoid a sensational engagement headline from a small study. For example, time spent in one math game is not a general learning multiplier. Confidence, enjoyment, completion, knowledge, transfer, and actual civic behavior are distinct outcomes.

## Translate the research into a learning design

The prototype should make the act of understanding evidence part of what the player does. A lecture followed by unrelated collectibles would leave the learning outside the game. The following mappings are proposed design decisions informed by the literature, not experimentally proven features of this prototype.

| Learning goal | Playable decision | Evidence of understanding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use the correct denominator | Repair a printing mechanism with SV, NV, and NS bins; produce a source-labelled public bulletin. | Select SV + NV in a new case and explain why unknown classification remains separate. |
| Distinguish counts and rates | Repair two same-year state noticeboards and combine their counts. | Recognize that a place can have more marked-vote records but a lower rate; calculate the combined rate correctly. |
| Know the limits of aggregates | Construct “El dato muestra”, “No permite saber”, and “Faltan datos” panels. | Reject a motive, individual-behavior, or causal assertion without dismissing the supported descriptive pattern. |
| Understand collective choice | Take part or decline in a clearly fictional community decision with fixed rules and a finite budget. | Explain that votes contribute to the count, need not change the winner individually, and do not guarantee good implementation. |
| Transfer the lesson | Resolve a later unseen chart or scenario without the original dialogue cues. | Give a source-backed explanation and a neutral accountability question, not merely recall the game’s wording. |

### Feedback that teaches

When the player makes a mistake, identify the mistaken assumption and show the relevant evidence. Offer a retry and an optional hint. Keep a separate assisted-completion flag so the evaluation does not mistake repeated guessing for independent understanding. A later retrieval opportunity checks the idea in a different example. [3, 5–6]

### Autonomy and neutral civic framing

Do not reward a particular ballot choice, shame abstention, or derive an NPC’s preferences from a real demographic average. Present legitimate competing fictional needs. An accessible invitation or cleared path can visibly improve the fictional event without claiming that it causes a quantified rise in real turnout. Label all simulation outcomes as fictional.

### Questions the existing research does not answer

- Does this particular 10–15 minute adventure improve CCPC interpretation among its intended audience?

- Does any gain persist after 7–14 days and transfer to an unfamiliar question?

- Does the visual adventure add value beyond the same content in a well-designed, time-matched nongame lesson?

- Can people with different gaming familiarity and access needs complete the experience without avoidable barriers?

## A realistic evaluation roadmap

### Before the hackathon deadline

If capacity permits, run a formative usability check with a target of eight adult volunteers, with at least five as a pragmatic minimum. Include varied gaming familiarity and seek accessibility feedback. These are proposed recruitment targets, not participants already enrolled or data already collected. Do not delay required deliverables to pursue a small efficacy claim.

- Observe a complete route without coaching. Record where a person gets stuck, misunderstands a chart, requests help, or cannot operate a control.

- Use a few alternate pre/post or transfer items to diagnose confusing content. A small uncontrolled increase is descriptive feedback, not causal evidence.

- Fix severe interaction or explanation failures, then retest the change. If no volunteers are available, report internal testing only.

- Optional formative target: four of five can complete the core route within roughly 15 minutes without moderator rescue and explain two data limitations. This is a team-set diagnostic criterion, not a validated educational benchmark.

### After the hackathon

Propose a randomized feasibility study of approximately 60 consenting adults, allocated 1:1 to the game or a time-matched nongame lesson with the same facts and charts. This sample is for feasibility, not a claim of adequate power for definitive efficacy. Keep devices, facilitator contact, and exposure time comparable. If assignment is by class, account for clustering rather than treating students as independent.

Prespecify a primary outcome: an alternate-form civic and data-literacy score at 7–14 days, adjusted for baseline score. Assess immediately after the session as a secondary learning measure. Include unseen transfer scenarios scored with a rubric by reviewers unaware of condition when practical. A later confirmatory study needs a power analysis with attrition and clustering assumptions.

### Report the result regardless of direction

Record the protocol and scoring before examining outcomes. Report sample size by group, missing follow-up, baseline scores, means and standard deviations, adjusted group differences, 95% confidence intervals, and standardized effect sizes. Describe recruitment and implementation failures. Do not cherry-pick a subgroup, substitute engagement for learning, or treat a nonsignificant result as proof of no possible effect.

### Privacy and participant care

Use opt-in participation, anonymous session IDs, a clear withdrawal/reset route, and minimal data. Do not collect voter ID, precise address, ballot preference, party affiliation, or political persuasion profiles. Do not persist the fictional ballot choice in the game save. Keep research consent and any follow-up contact separate from gameplay records; define retention and access before collection.

## Measurements and example assessment items

| Measure | Operational definition | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary delayed learning | Baseline-adjusted alternate-form score at 7–14 days in the later randomized feasibility study. | Estimates retained learning under the study conditions; not real-world turnout. |
| Immediate learning | Correct answers by concept immediately after the activity. | Can reveal short-term learning or test familiarity; keep separate from delayed retention. |
| Transfer | Two unseen scenarios; 0–2 points each for a correct conclusion and a defensible explanation. | Tests use beyond memorized dialogue; predefine examples and blinded scoring. |
| Usability | Completion, navigation errors, help requests, accessibility blockers, and recovery. | A complete route is necessary but does not establish learning. |
| Engagement | Voluntary replay choice, active time excluding idle time, and a brief enjoyment response. | More time can reflect interest or confusion. Do not equate it with better learning. |
| Confidence calibration | Confidence compared with answer correctness. | Higher confidence alone is not success; overconfidence can be a negative outcome. |

### Example items in Mexican Spanish

1. “En un ejemplo didáctico hay 60 registros con marca, 30 sin marca y 10 sin clasificación. ¿Qué registros entran en el denominador de la tasa de participación?” Correct reasoning: 60 + 30; the numbers are synthetic, not INE observations.

2. “Un lugar tiene más registros con marca de voto, pero una tasa menor. ¿Es posible? Explica qué dato te falta para comprobarlo.” Correct reasoning: yes; compare the classified denominators and calculate both rates.

3. “La tasa de un grupo fue menor. ¿Podemos concluir que cada persona de ese grupo tenía menos interés?” Correct reasoning: no; the aggregate does not identify individual behavior or motives.

4. “Tu opción no ganó en la consulta ficticia. ¿Qué aportó tu voto y qué podrías preguntar después?” Correct reasoning: it entered the count; a neutral question can examine implementation, spending, or unresolved needs.

### Scoring and integrity

Use parallel forms with new values and wording, not identical pre/post questions. For explanations, award 0 for unsupported or absent reasoning, 1 for a correct conclusion with incomplete rationale, and 2 for correct reasoning plus the relevant limitation. Have two reviewers resolve ambiguous responses. If source-checking items include misleading content, include accurate content too and score discrimination, not blanket distrust.

Any procedural statement about Mexican voting rights, secrecy, registration, or institutional roles must be reviewed against current official INE guidance before use. The prototype is not a full constitutional election simulator. No policy, registration, or procedural rule should be inferred from the toy consultation.

## Pitch outline slides 1 to 5

The official limit is 10 slides. The outline below uses concise Spanish on-screen copy and English presenter notes. It is a script structure, not a finished deck. Replace all “planned” language only when the corresponding feature has actually been implemented and tested.

### 1 The data and the question

Spanish copy: “Participar también requiere comprender la información.”
“CCPC 2024: 59.7% entre registros clasificados; 6.4% sin clasificación en la lista nominal analizada.”
Visual: one clean labelled data card, not a dramatic age-ranking chart.
English notes: Define both denominators. The data describes historical participation; it does not establish why people did not vote. Cite INE’s June 2025 report and the reproducible ZIP calculation. [11–12]

### 2 The proposed experience

Spanish copy: “Voces de la Plaza”
“Una aventura 2D breve para investigar, construir evidencia y participar en una decisión colectiva.”
Visual: original town concept or actual prototype screenshot, accurately labelled.
English notes: Identify the audience as the general public and facilitators. The cozy style is a creative engagement hypothesis, not a research-proven intervention.

### 3 The learning is the action

Spanish copy: “Explora. Escucha. Comprueba. Decide. Explica.”
“Repara un cartel usando datos reales y explica lo que no podemos concluir.”
Visual: the actual printing or noticeboard interaction.
English notes: Show a complete decision-feedback-retry loop. Explain the persistent exhibit as a visible consequence of evidence quality, not a quiz score disguised as town prosperity. [3, 5]

### 4 CCPC is central

Spanish copy: “SV ÷ (SV + NV)”
“Sin clasificación no significa abstención.”
“Cada cifra conserva año, denominador y fuente.”
Visual: one verified evidence card with count and rate contrast.
English notes: Use the Aguascalientes/Baja California pair from the same year and universe. Show how a source record drives the interaction and how the build reproduces it. Keep synthetic examples visibly separate. [11–12]

### 5 Why the vote matters

Spanish copy: “Tu voto se cuenta. Tu opción no siempre gana.”
“La participación es libre y el seguimiento continúa después.”
“Consulta ficticia, con reglas y límites visibles.”
Visual: neutral fictional project options and aggregate results.
English notes: Explain finite resources, plural needs, privacy, and continued accountability. Completion does not depend on casting a ballot or selecting the winner. Turnout alone cannot predict the winning project.

## Pitch outline slides 6 to 10

### 6 The evidence behind the design

Spanish copy: “Los videojuegos educativos pueden apoyar el aprendizaje.”
“g = 0.33 en 57 muestras; IC 95% [0.19, 0.48].”
“El apoyo didáctico importa: d = 0.34.”
Visual: two restrained evidence statements with source footnotes.
English notes: Cite Clark et al. [1] and Wouters and van Oostendorp [3]. These are standardized differences from prior research, not percentages or results of this prototype. If mentioning Harmony Square [8], label it civic-adjacent and do not imply turnout effects.

### 7 A neutral and accessible experience

Spanish copy: “Sin partidos, perfiles políticos ni premios por una opción.”
“Teclado, texto ampliable y alternativa lineal accesible.”
“Datos reales y escenarios ficticios, siempre separados.”
Visual: tested settings and semantic text/table companion.
English notes: Demonstrate only options actually tested. A Godot canvas is not automatically screen-reader accessible. Show the accessible equivalent separately, and label touch or additional platforms as future work if not tested.

### 8 What we will measure

Spanish copy: “Primero: comprensión y facilidad de uso.”
“Después: comparación con una lección equivalente y retención a 7–14 días.”
“No prometemos aumentar la participación electoral sin evidencia.”
Visual: a simple planned evaluation sequence, without invented result bars.
English notes: Near-term formative target: eight adults if feasible, minimum five with limits. Later feasibility proposal: about 60 adults, randomized 1:1. Neither study is completed by this plan or guaranteed to establish efficacy.

### 9 Feasible delivery and reuse

Spanish copy: “Godot y contenido editable.”
“Datos CCPC verificados, ejecución local y sin cuenta obligatoria.”
“Guía de facilitación, fuentes y pruebas reproducibles.”
Visual: simple data-to-content-to-game flow plus tested delivery modes.
English notes: Codex helps development only; no runtime AI is needed. Show the rubric traceability and maintenance path. Explain private evaluator access pending clarification of originality and publication conditions.

### 10 The next step

Spanish copy: “Probar, corregir y documentar antes de ampliar.”
“Una herramienta de educación cívica para evaluar y adaptar.”
Visual: one final plaza image and a concise next-step list.
English notes: Ask for the opportunity to evaluate or refine the prototype, not claim institutional adoption. State tested scope, known limits, and the proposed pilot. Keep the complete citation list in the accompanying document.

Suggested video allocation within the 3-minute limit: 20 seconds for context, 100–110 seconds for a clearly labelled demonstration, 25–30 seconds for evidence and evaluation, and 15–20 seconds for feasibility and closing. Rehearse against the actual exported build; do not speed up footage until text becomes unreadable.
